Tap an animal lineage to replay its most prolific member in the 3D view, or a plant lineage to see its largest individual full-grown. Plants and animals share one graph genome. A node's form is continuous: a flatness gene runs from a round limb or stem to a thin sheet (wing or leaf), a grip gene from skin to a clawed foot, and stiffness and freedom genes set each joint from welded to a free ball; every physical quantity blends on them. Life starts in the sea as floating algae sheets and nothing else: heterotrophy has to arise, first as absorption of dead matter through the surface (a heterotrophy gene that displaces photosynthesis, the mixotroph's trade-off), then motility (small drift with the occasional flagellum-sized jump), and only then mouths; gut type, weapons, armour, body size, fat reserves, offspring size, predation, scavenging, stems, roots and land tolerance all evolve. Diet labels are what an animal actually ate, not a gene. A motility gene decides kingdom: offspring above 0.5 are born motile (animals), below it sessile (plants), so lineages can cross over in either direction. Plants reach land through a land-tolerance gene and the wet shore; herbivores follow because shore-dwellers can graze adjacent land plants from the water, better the more they can crawl, and every new body is measured on land as well as in water. Species are lineages: a child with a changed body plan starts a new lineage with some probability. The table shows the top ten per kind under the filter above it: habitat is where most of a lineage's members live (the shore is the intertidal band, under water half the day), "Gliders & fliers" are lineages whose bodies glide at under 45° (ten metres beyond a thrown stone from ten metres up) or stay aloft, and the replay picks a member from the filtered habitat. Wings are tested on any body light enough for them to matter (wing loading under 50 kg/m²): launched at 3 m/s from 10 m, metres beyond where a stone lands are the glide; still aloft at the end is flight. A glider uses its patch's canopy and hillsides as launch height to move between patches, escape ground predators and cover ground; a flier ranges over many patches a day, crosses water, surveys carrion from above and is beyond reach of anything on the ground.

Derived physics

Derived ecology

Illustrative Earth-normalised model: productivity from light, CO₂, temperature and water; animal cost from gravity and buoyancy; oxygen caps metabolic rate.

How the physics ties to the dials
  • Gravity: weight grows with volume × g; muscle torque with limb cross-section. Heavy worlds favour short, thick limbs.
  • Pressure, O₂, CO₂, temperature set air density by the ideal gas law. Every membrane sub-panel gets a flat-plate normal force F = ½ρA|v|²·2.61·sin α plus parasitic drag; limbs get anisotropic quadratic drag.
  • O₂ partial pressure feeds an oxygen budget: lung fraction × torso volume × pO₂ against muscle section × frequency. The ratio scales motor strength (fatigue factor).
  • Humidity and temperature set aridity. Motor strength on land is multiplied by hydration unless the water-store gene covers the need; stored water adds mass.
  • Water adds Archimedes buoyancy from tissue volume and submersion damping; the same lift code runs at water density, so fins and wings share physics.
  • Measurement, not scoring: every new body runs a short battery (land and water with motors on and off, steering and posture shifts, an air launch if it is light enough). Sustained speed after settling, net of the motors-off twin, becomes the body's range and its chances in a chase. Nothing is ranked; the ecology decides what a body is worth.
  • Everything runs at a fixed 120 Hz step. Runs that go non-finite or exceed 60 m/s are discarded, because evolution exploits any energy leak.
